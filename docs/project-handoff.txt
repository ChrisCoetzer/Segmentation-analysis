I’m working on a graduate-level data visualization / analytics consulting project using the Criteo Uplift Prediction dataset as the required primary dataset, with the Criteo Attribution Modeling dataset available as a secondary source.

The class structure is that each team acts as a boutique analytics consultancy. We are not given a fixed task. We have to identify the client, define a decision worth changing, justify why the data can support it, build an interactive Streamlit application, and present the work in a short five-slide pitch and final demo.

The main challenge is that the Criteo uplift dataset is unusually large and experimentally strong, but its customer features are anonymized. That makes a normal “identify the best customer segment” dashboard much less useful than it first appears.

## Current Project Direction

The primary question we are pursuing is:

**How much value is actually available from customer segmentation and personalized targeting?**

The uplift dataset can show whether advertising response differs across customer profiles, but the features `f0`–`f11` are anonymized and transformed. We therefore cannot say things like:

- “mobile users respond better”
- “recent visitors should be targeted”
- “high-income users are persuadable”
- “repeat buyers should be suppressed”

Even if a model finds a high-uplift group, the released data does not tell us what that group means in normal marketing terms.

Because of that, our direction is not:

> “Who are the best customer segments?”

It is:

> **“Is there enough difference in customer response to advertising that discovering and operationalizing those segments would actually be worth investing in?”**

This is a more defensible use of the data.

## Proposed Client

A good client is:

**VP of Marketing Analytics / Head of Customer Strategy at a large e-commerce advertiser**

The organization is considering investing in:

- deeper customer segmentation

## Business Problem

Many marketing organizations assume that more segmentation and personalization are automatically better.

But segmentation has costs:

- data collection
- research
- model development
- tooling
- maintenance
- creative variation
- campaign complexity
- measurement complexity

The client needs evidence that meaningful response differences actually exist before committing to that investment.

The client question is:

**Is deeper segmentation/personalization worth investing in, and how much additional performance is realistically available from it?**

The decision the app should change is:

**How much should the organization invest in discovering and operationalizing distinct customer segments versus continuing with a broader targeting strategy?**

## Working Mandate

Build an interactive decision-support tool that quantifies how much incremental advertising performance becomes available as customer segmentation becomes more sophisticated.

The app should compare:

1. treating the whole population as one group
2. very coarse segmentation
3. moderate segmentation
4. more advanced heterogeneous-treatment-effect targeting

The core concept is:

**Segmentation Headroom**

This means the additional incremental performance available from exploiting differences in treatment response rather than using one population-wide policy.

The key question is not whether the most sophisticated model wins by a tiny benchmark metric.

The question is:

> **How quickly do the gains from segmentation appear, and where do they start to flatten?**

Possible interpretations:

- If almost all the gain is captured with 2–4 broad groups, then expensive hyper-personalization may not be justified.
- If the curve keeps improving meaningfully as segmentation becomes more sophisticated, there may be substantial value in investing in deeper customer discovery.
- If there is almost no improvement over one broad treatment policy, then the business case for segmentation may be weak.

## Important Difference From a Standard Uplift Project

A teammate proposed a more conventional idea:

> build an incrementality dashboard that identifies which users should be targeted and which should not.

That is technically possible as an uplift-modeling exercise, but it overstates what the released data can support as a business tool.

We can estimate which anonymized feature profiles have higher or lower expected treatment effect.

However:

- we cannot identify what those profiles mean in normal marketing terms
- we cannot produce deployable rules such as “target recent visitors”
- we cannot know for a specific individual that they definitely would or would not have converted without the ad
- we only observe one potential outcome for each user

So a standard output such as:

> “users with high f3 and low f8 should be targeted”

is analytically valid but not very useful to a real marketing organization.

That is why the current project measures the **value of knowing the segments**, rather than pretending the public dataset tells us what the segments are.

## How the Experiment Works

This dataset comes from randomized advertising incrementality experiments.

Conceptually:

1. eligible users enter the experiment
2. users are randomly assigned to treatment or control
3. treatment users are eligible to receive advertising
4. some treated users are actually effectively exposed to an ad
5. later behavior is recorded
6. outcomes include visit and conversion

Important variables:

### `treatment`

`treatment = 1`
User was randomly assigned to the treatment group and was eligible to receive advertising.

`treatment = 0`
User was assigned to the control group and advertising was withheld.

This is the main causal treatment indicator.

### `exposure`

`exposure = 1`
The treated user was actually effectively exposed to advertising.

`exposure = 0`
The user was not effectively exposed.

A user can therefore have:

`treatment = 1`
`exposure = 0`

This means they were assigned to the treatment group but were never actually effectively exposed to an ad.

Do not treat `exposure` as the randomized treatment variable.

Primary causal comparisons should use `treatment`.

### `visit`

Binary indicator for whether the user visited the advertiser.

This is a relatively common outcome and is likely the best primary outcome for early treatment-effect and heterogeneity analysis.

### `conversion`

Binary indicator for whether the user completed the advertiser’s desired conversion action.

The precise business action is anonymized.

A conversion does not mean the ad caused the conversion.

Control users can convert as well.

The causal quantity comes from comparing treatment and control conversion rates.

## Counterfactual Limitation

For any individual user, we never observe both:

- what would happen if treated
- what would happen if untreated

We observe only one.

Therefore we cannot say:

> “This individual definitely converted because of the advertisement.”

We can estimate something more like:

> “Users with feature profiles like this appear to have a higher expected conversion probability under treatment than under control.”

This distinction matters in both the analysis and the UI language.

## Anonymization

The twelve customer features are intentionally anonymized.

They should be treated as transformed numeric coordinates that preserve useful predictive relationships but do not have recoverable business meanings.

Do not assume values such as:

`f0 = 12.62`

mean:

- 12.62%
- 12.62 times average
- a standardized z-score
- age
- recency
- spend
- any other interpretable marketing variable

The original semantic labels and units are gone.

This is a major reason for the current project direction.

## Non-Uniform Subsampling

The released uplift dataset was also non-uniformly subsampled for privacy.

This means the released public data should not be treated as a simple representative random sample of the advertiser’s original customer population.

Consequences:

- segment sizes in the public dataset may not equal real segment shares
- aggregate lift values should not be interpreted as the advertiser’s true production incrementality
- absolute treatment-effect levels may be affected by the release process
- the dataset remains useful as a large-scale benchmark for relative treatment-response structure and model comparison

Therefore the project should avoid statements such as:

> “All advertisers should expect X% improvement from segmentation.”

Safer language:

> “Within this large randomized Criteo benchmark, increasing segmentation sophistication captured X additional treatment-response structure.”

The project should be framed as an empirical benchmark of segmentation headroom, not a universal market estimate.

## Main Analytical Question

The first important analytical question is:

**Does this dataset contain meaningful and stable heterogeneity in advertising response, or is most of the treatment effect adequately summarized by one average effect?**

This must be answered before fully committing to the segmentation-headroom story.

Do not assume meaningful heterogeneity exists.

Test it.

If the dataset does not show stable out-of-sample heterogeneity, surface that result rather than forcing the original hypothesis.

## Proposed Analysis Pipeline

### Step 1 — Data Validation

Confirm:

- row count
- schema
- dtypes
- missing values
- duplicates if relevant
- treatment/control proportions
- exposure rates
- visit rate
- conversion rate
- feature distributions
- memory footprint

Do not alter the raw dataset unnecessarily.

### Step 2 — Average Treatment Effects

For both `visit` and `conversion`, calculate:

- treatment outcome rate
- control outcome rate
- absolute treatment effect
- relative treatment effect
- standard error
- confidence interval
- statistical significance where appropriate

This becomes the “no segmentation” baseline.

### Step 3 — Simple Heterogeneity Exploration

Before using complex ML, inspect whether treatment response varies across the feature space.

Possible approaches:

- quantile bins for each feature
- treatment effect by feature decile
- confidence intervals within groups
- shallow decision trees
- simple interaction terms
- two-dimensional feature slices where justified

The goal is not to create personas.

The goal is to determine whether different regions of the feature space have meaningfully different causal responses.

### Step 4 — Segmentation Complexity Ladder

Build a transparent sequence from simple to more sophisticated.

Possible structure:

**Level 0**
No segmentation.
One average treatment effect.

**Level 1**
Very coarse segmentation.
For example 2–4 groups.

**Level 2**
Moderate segmentation.
For example 5–10 groups or shallow tree leaves.

**Level 3**
One flexible heterogeneous treatment-effect model.

Candidate models could include:

- S-learner
- T-learner
- shallow uplift/causal tree
- LightGBM/XGBoost-based model
- transformed-outcome approach

Do not turn this into a giant algorithm competition.

The model is a tool for measuring segmentation value, not the final product.

### Step 5 — Honest Held-Out Evaluation

This is critical.

Do not discover segments and evaluate them on the same data.

Use:

- train
- validation
- test

or another honest sample-splitting approach.

Any claimed increase in segmentation value must be measured on data not used to create the segmentation.

Actively test for overfitting.

### Step 6 — Segmentation Headroom Curve

This is likely the main chart.

Possible x-axis:

- no segmentation
- 2 groups
- 4 groups
- 8 groups
- 16 groups
- flexible model

Possible y-axis:

- incremental visits captured
- incremental conversions captured
- estimated policy value
- percentage of available treatment effect captured
- another defensible out-of-sample incremental metric

The shape of this curve is the core business result.

We care about diminishing returns.

### Step 7 — Targeting-Efficiency Curve

Another useful visual:

X-axis:
percentage of customers targeted

Y-axis:
percentage of incremental outcome captured

Compare:

- broad/random policy
- coarse segmentation
- more sophisticated targeting

This answers something like:

> “How much of the incremental response could we preserve while advertising to fewer users?”

This can help translate treatment heterogeneity into a marketing-efficiency story.

## Main Outcome Choice

`visit` should probably be the primary outcome during model development.

Reason:

- it has far more positive observations
- subgroup treatment effects will be much more stable
- conversion is very rare

Approximate released-data rates previously observed/documented are around:

- visit: ~4.7%
- conversion: ~0.29%

At 0.29% conversion, approximate positive counts are:

100,000 rows:
~290 conversions

500,000 rows:
~1,450 conversions

1,000,000 rows:
~2,900 conversions

5,000,000 rows:
~14,500 conversions

13,979,592 rows:
~40,000 conversions

This is why conversion-level subgroup analysis becomes unstable very quickly on small samples.

Use conversion as a secondary business-relevant outcome once the pipeline is stable.

## Why This Is Different From Existing Work

The original Criteo uplift papers largely use the dataset as a benchmark for uplift modeling / individual treatment-effect estimation.

Common questions in the literature are:

- Which uplift model performs best?
- How should uplift models be evaluated?
- How much data is needed to distinguish model performance?
- Which model has the best Qini/AUUC?
- How accurately can heterogeneous treatment effects be predicted?

Public projects also commonly:

- train S-learners
- train T-learners
- compare X/R/DR learners
- calculate Qini curves
- rank persuadable users
- simulate top-uplift targeting

Our project is deliberately different.

The model is not the final answer.

The model is used to answer a higher-level consulting question:

**How much business value is actually available from increasing segmentation sophistication?**

The project asks whether the organization should invest in discovering/operationalizing customer differences before assuming that more personalization is automatically worthwhile.
