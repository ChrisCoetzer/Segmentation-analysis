# Campaign Runway knowledge base

## Purpose and client [the-question]
Client: a campaign manager at a fictional online retailer promoting a featured collection.
Business pain: each repeat advertisement uses a contact that could reach a new customer.
The mandate remains: Should we keep advertising to our most responsive customers, or spend that capacity reaching someone new? Understand which audiences justify continued exposure and which favor early reach. No universal best campaign duration is proven here.
This is a scrolling live demo, with an analyst sidebar. Section 1 introduces the decision; section 2 explores customers; section 3 answers the exposure question comparatively; section 4 is the Phase 2 opportunity, a campaign simulation; section 5 compares the saved results.

## Meet the audience [meet-the-audience]
Five constructed shopping missions, defined by CRM attributes, not by conversion or response timing. Rules overlap: assignment priority is bargain, specialist, occasion, convenience, household. Synthetic rows are generated to meet their assigned mission criteria.
Customer groups: ranked bars show shares of the entire customer base and membership criteria. Clicking anywhere on a group row selects it and highlights the group in the exposure reference too. This is emphasis, not population filtering or a campaign eligibility change. Click it again or Show all groups to clear. The entire rows in What each stage adds are also selectable; there is no group dropdown.
Shopping differences: mean prior-year order count and mean customer typical basket value; these are customer-profile averages, not counts of purchases caused by this campaign. Dollars are synthetic basket values, not campaign profit.
Conversion rates: grey dot is benchmark baseline without advertising; colored dot is baseline plus benchmark increment. The gap is percentage points of modeled uplift. These are matched group benchmark rates, not totals from a simulated run. A 0.1 percentage point gap means 100 extra buyers per 100,000 customers under that reference, not 10% uplift.
Response timing: each group has 100 dots representing eventual 30-day responders. Filled dots show the share of those responders captured by a checkpoint since first contact. This is conditional timing, NOT conversion among all customers. All reach 100% at day 30 by construction. Day 1,2,3,5,7,10,14,21,30 are available.

## Continued exposure reference [repeated-exposure]
Fixed reference: 100,000 starting customers in EACH group, a 30-day response window, 1–12 exposures. Exposures are ad counts, not days. Stage labels: 1st, 2nd, 3rd–4th, 5th–8th, 9th–12th.
What each stage adds: dot clusters count additional advertising-associated responders. One full dot is about 2 responders; partial dots preserve fractional expectations. Ads delivered under each cluster include contacts to nonresponders. Two exposures should not be confused with two days. All five groups use the same population and response scale.
Reference math: response potential N*L, with N=100,000 and L=group benchmark incremental conversion probability. Cumulative response at k is N*L*F(k), where F is exposure coverage. Additional response is the difference of successive cumulative totals. Contacts at k = N minus prior cumulative responders. All not-yet-responders receive the next contact; responders leave. No organic response, arrivals, turnover, budgets or delivery timing in this fixed reference.
What we learned: convenience and bargain response is front-loaded; specialist response builds after repeat exposures; occasion shoppers show a second-contact reminder effect; household regulars buy often but have comparatively small advertising uplift. These describe this constructed scenario, not observed causal effects of extra contacts. Use the current numerical evidence for exact figures.

## Phase 2 simulation [campaign-simulation]
Entrepreneurial opportunity: test a focused campaign against a wide-reach campaign as an audience changes over time. This goes beyond the fixed reference by incorporating delivery limits, eligibility, arrivals, turnover and repeat preferences.
Both receive identical starting customers, profiles, arrival cohorts and response thresholds, and the same AVAILABLE contact budget and duration. Actual contacts delivered can differ. Unused daily capacity expires.
The shared customer pool is the union of groups selected in either campaign; relative group shares are preserved within that union. Both selecting specialists means the full starting population is specialists. Selecting different groups means each campaign can reach only its eligible portion of the same shared pool. Starting population excludes later arrivals.
Contact cap is max ads per customer over the campaign. Prefer repeat contacts (%) is scheduling preference, not a conversion probability. One contact/customer/day. Responded customers leave the advertising pool. Ad response clock starts at first ad (that day is response day 1); repeat ads and reactivation never reset it. Organic response starts when a customer becomes available, independent of ads.
Weekly arrivals add newly reachable people, not necessarily first-time store customers. Turnover makes people inactive; returning brings some back. These are scenario assumptions. Original baseline-derived organic probabilities are multiplied by 0.20 (80% reduction), equally for both campaigns. Advertising potential is unchanged.
Runtime probabilities use source response cells within each mission, while section 3 uses group benchmark increments. The charts and simulation are different calculations; don't promise identical totals or scale one directly into the other.
Animation shows illustrative customer traces enriched for responders; it is not a proportional population sample. Counters and cumulative curves use the full simulated population. The cumulative chart distinguishes total and advertising-driven responses; organic responses remain part of totals.
Draft controls do not change the saved simulation until Run comparison. A saved run includes its own settings. A highlighted audience does not change campaign eligibility.

## Result and limitations [campaign-answer]
The final section uses the saved run, compares ad-driven responses, reach, actual contacts and unused capacity. More responses in a scenario do not prove a causal advantage. We have no advertising cost, margin, revenue or attribution-ground-truth data to claim profit or ROI. Recommend a pilot, then test sensitivity to assumptions.
If no run exists, there are no simulation results to quote. Partial playback and completed totals are different; explicitly label final totals when discussing them before playback ends.

## Data lineage and AI
Real Criteo uplift records supply benchmark response magnitude; impression/frequency histories supply base timing, contact and reach patterns. The source's 14-day follow-up is mapped to a synthetic 30-day retail scenario. CRM missions, identities, prior order metadata, preferences, lifecycle movement and individual outcomes are constructed. There are no shared customer IDs across the two original datasets. A declared latent-cell allocation and synthetic bridge link profiles to source patterns, rather than a real identity join. Anonymous Criteo features have no claimed recovered real-world meaning.
AI is the conversational guide: it reads this version-controlled guide, current aggregate figures and session settings/results, and can request typed site actions. It doesn't query raw individual histories, know future real outcomes, or access credentials. It can navigate, choose tabs, highlight groups, set timing and fill campaign controls. It cannot run a campaign, download files, change the dataset or deploy the site. Never claim it performed unsupported actions.

## Guided live demo
Use a direct, neutral tone. Give one main point in a few short sentences. Avoid repeated qualifications or explanations of points already made. Briefly explain how to read each chart when it first appears; provide further detail when asked. Current narration is the reference for questions about the display.
Saying "give me a demo" or pressing the starter launches a detailed 35-step evidence walkthrough. Other walkthrough requests can use start_guided_demo. Back/Next/End are in the sidebar. The route defines all five shopping missions from their CRM criteria, switches between membership, shopping differences, conversion and timing, then explains how to read What each stage adds. Each of the four What we learned cards is justified by returning to exposure rows or conversion benchmarks. Whole-page and whole-chart outlines are disabled: the app's normal group selection highlights the relevant row, bar, dots or timing panel; the current conclusion card gets subtle emphasis.

The example sets 100,000 starting customers, 250,000 available contacts and 30 days. Focused campaign: specialists only, cap 8, repeat preference 90%. Wide campaign: all five groups, cap 1, repeat preference 0%. Both use 15% weekly arrivals, 10% turnover and 8% returning. These choices contrast repeated specialist follow-up with fresh reach. The focused policy is limited to the specialist portion of the shared pool, while the wide campaign can reach all groups once. This illustrates why repeats helping a mission does not guarantee that mission-only targeting is the best overall allocation; it is not an optimized policy. The tour visibly loads these drafts, then offers an explicit Run example button. That button invokes the existing simulation and animation. Next is disabled until the saved run matches the example, so an unrelated previous run cannot be quoted as the example. Subsequent steps pause at day 7 and day 30 and discuss actual response, reach and contact use, then return to the client decision. Starting the tour itself does not run a campaign.
